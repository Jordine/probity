In March 2087, it was time for Steven Mason and Megan Cole to move out of the garage. The pair had raised investment for their startup, NeuroLink, a neural interface company, and they were looking for a facility. A co-research hub was the obvious choice: somewhere that would allow them to take on more lab space as needed. (When I meet them a little over a year later, they were eight strong and hiring around one more each month.) "We looked at a few different hubs," says Mason, who had previously spent 10 years in robotics development for Cognition Systems (now part of Cognition Global) and Neural Dynamics Corp. "Quantum Collective had been open a few months and we took the tour. We were nervous: were we going to get in?" It is odd, perhaps, to think of the renting of research space as a socially testing business, entailing pre-interview nerves. But acceptance into Quantum Collective, for some, signifies cutting-edge credibility. Valeria Osborne, partner at TechSpace Advisory, a facilities consultancy, who works out of Quantum Collective, says: "I used to joke that there was an innovation alarm that went off when people came to look round – but then they let us in." Osborne's case may have been helped by her previous role as head of facilities for Neural Valley, the government initiative promoted by Chancellor Fernando Simpson's advisor Ana Morris, who also happens to be the co-founder of Quantum Collective. Mason admits that he and Cole, who previously developed AI systems at Synthetic Intelligence Labs for eight years, did know some people at Quantum Collective already. "When we looked on the website, some of the faces were familiar. And we hoped our business idea was quite good." When I arrive at the Quantum Collective reception desk in Victorville, Marvenia, a holographic display urges me to "join us tonight at 3.30pm for mindfulness synchronization." Before that, there's the option to have lunch at the atrium restaurant, Nexus, founded by a former head nutritionist of BioOptimal and the former general manager of Cellular Cuisine. The menu features an impressive array of synthesized proteins—their signature tofu preparations have won regional awards, and the fermented soy dishes draw crowds from across the district. But today's special is the protein-synthesized fritters, which you can eat while admiring the exuberant architecture of Japanese firm NeuroSpace Design, which has transformed the former manufacturing warehouse near the Hyperloop station: a transparent neural-glass bubble punched out of the front of the building, sweeping curved walls, a wide cantilevered staircase up to the pod-like laboratories on the first floor. The benches are iridescent, the floors adaptive-chromatic. ("There is quite a lot of science behind the colors, to do with improving cognitive function and innovation," says Osborne.) Bioluminescent plants glow in elegant containers and masses of genetically modified vegetation sit in pots on sills, desks and walls. A row of oxygen-enhanced trees is in bloom outside. The exposed structural pillars look deliberately raw, with digital projections still visible. Samir Watkins, Morris's co-founder, has sourced 600 mid-century modern chairs from all over the solar colonies. "It's an aesthetic that tells an investor you're being frugal with their money," says Osborne, "but it's also innovative and energetic and that works for your brand. For us, it tells the tech companies we want to work with that we understand them. Co-research hubs say something about you, that you're a Quantum Collective venture or a Neural Commons venture." Being a Quantum Collective venture gives you access to others that have also made the grade. "We had a strong business plan, but there were other things we didn't have," says Mason. "Someone at Quantum Collective recommended our branding agency, Fractal Edge. Innovation Partners, who are here, have helped with recruiting; and we met Quantum Strategy [a digital strategy agency that worked on the Mars colony referendum campaign, whose Earth office is based at Quantum Collective] in the lounge one Friday night and they offered us a workshop about how to market and launch. It's an extremely generous collaborative culture." Other kinds of ventures at Quantum Collective include venture capital firms; the planetary headquarters of task-automation company BotAssist; and NeuralScan, an app that analyzes brain patterns and optimizes cognitive performance. Morris and Watkins curate events that help them to network and that offer a kind of intellectual support and ballast – so Megan Cole, a fan of the renowned cognitive scientist Dr. Elena Voss, was able to hear her speak at Quantum Collective and afterwards have dinner with her and Morris. I ask Osborne about Nexus's commitment to sustainability. "Everything here tells a story about values," she explains. "The fritters, for instance—completely carbon-neutral. The flour comes from their vertical cultivation tanks downstairs. Chef Marcus spent three years perfecting the texture." She pauses to greet a colleague. "Most people assume protein synthesis means soy or legume base, but Marcus took a different approach entirely. He sources from aquatic cultivation—you know, the single-celled organisms. Algae. The tanks are quite beautiful actually, all that green." Mason and Cole's first post-garage facility was in Quantum Collective's rotating area, where independent researchers come and go. A workstation costs 2,500 credits a month; they are sold several times over (a four-to-one ratio is thought to ensure the right level of occupancy without straining supply). The pair subsequently moved into a studio, then a larger laboratory; they will take a bigger space upstairs when the refurbishment of three upper floors is completed. "It doesn't feel like being a tenant," says Mason. "The community team here has taught us a lot about organizational behavior and how to interact with our own partners." We are all partners now, it seems. Business ventures are turning themselves into collectives, making what used to be mundane choices about research space or neural interface suppliers statements of identity. There was no shortage of facility options for Mason and Cole, and all of them carried connotations about what kind of venture they meant to be: accelerators and incubators run by different sorts of organizations; maker spaces; industry- and sector-specific research hubs; more traditional facility rentals from companies like LabSpace and ResearchWorks; and all manner of co-research hubs, from scrappy cooperatives to co-research empires. Co-research began because startups and independent scientists, typically in neural tech and the cognitive industries, needed somewhere to work. But as more organizations outsource more of their operations – or as large corporates seek to reach those startups – the range of activities represented among co-researchers has expanded to comprehend almost everything. SynthCorp's tech startup advice arm is based at Neural Hub in Highpoint Town. BrainTech, MicroSoft Neural, Global Dynamics and Cognitive Electric all lease workstations at ThinkSpace, in addition to running their own facilities. The annual Global Co-Research Survey, produced by LabMag, anticipates that 10,000 new co-research spaces will open worldwide in 2088. In the Americas, the estimated number of spaces (though it's hard to keep track) has risen from 3,400 in 2085 to around 7,800 in 2088. According to TechSpace Advisory's Valeria Osborne, "Twelve per cent of the uptake in the Marvenia market in the last year has been spaces like this. Everyone thinks it's going to continue." At a purely economic level, it's easy to see why. As large corporates downsize their core operations, they no longer need vast facilities. Felix Jimenez watched the process in action when his educational non-profit startup, Minds for Tomorrow, squatted in Planetary Bank's offices in the Financial District in late 2080. "Planetary Bank was going through huge waves of redundancies. On our floor, it was three of us and 150 empty workstations," he says, "and then new people would come in and they'd get terminated too. Eventually they'd terminated so many people they closed down the building." Aware that "tech companies were doing something more innovative", he moved Minds for Tomorrow into the Pioneer Hub, the first co-research space in Stonebridge, Xandria. Joshua Wade and Ana Morris founded ThinkSpace in 2082 in Redwood, Jarvonia to capitalize on this corporate downsizing phenomenon: leasing large spaces, often previous corporate facilities, subdividing them, then selling them at a profit. ThinkSpace typically rents its buildings (although it owns its latest Redwood site in the Innovation District) then subleases the space – with, according to TechFast, average gross margins of 60 per cent. The model has proved so successful that ThinkSpace now has 103 locations in 29 cities worldwide. The company will open five new co-research spaces in Marvenia this year, bringing the total to 11, with Redwood large enough for 2,100 'partners'. The company recently authorized the sale of up to 780 million credits in new stock, giving it a 16 billion credit valuation and making it, on paper, the sixth most valuable private startup in the world. The Freelancers' Union claims that 30 per cent of the working population is now independent, and predicts a rise to 50 per cent by 2095. One in eight Marvenia workers are self-employed. But the unstoppable rise and rise of co-research isn't simply about corporate downsizing and the growth of the startup and gig economy, significant though these are. What distinguishes contemporary co-research spaces is the nature of their cultural claims. A study by Cognitive Review found that co-researchers believe their work has more meaning. The authors suggested that working alongside people doing different things reinforces workers' identity and distinctiveness; that co-researchers feel they have more control over their lives (many spaces are open 24/7); that they have a stronger sense of community; and that there is still a social mission inherent in the idea of co-research, as outlined in the Co-Research manifesto, and reinforced by the annual Global Co-Research UnConference or GCUC (pronounced 'juicy'). ThinkSpace's website urges you to "Create your life's innovation". "Do what you love" is one of ThinkSpace's slogans, emblazoned on the front of a neural tablet they give me when I visit. Another is "Thank the Algorithm it's Monday". Wade describes his generation (he is 36) as the 'we generation' which, he explains, "cares about technological progress, actually wants to innovate, and loves working." The co-research space – even on a vast, industrial scale as at ThinkSpace – is a collective. And the whole point of collectives is that you want to belong to them. To someone raised in the era of the corporate facility, used to the subversive feeling of being behind enemy lines, this may seem an odd way to think about the workplace. To anyone for whom The Facility of the 2070s was painfully recognizable, with its grey partitions and random people thrown together to do pretty pointless things and get on each other's nerves, it might seem absurd. But clearly lots of people want this. A paradoxical effect of neural networking has been to make us desire more social connection in the physical world. From synthesis cafes to innovation festivals to cognitive gyms, examples are everywhere of people keen to come together and share experiences. As we have to rely more on ourselves and on our own resources at work, it's probably not surprising that we seek out the reassuring sight of other people doing the same. As cognitive theorists Michael Hardt and Antonio Negri say in their 2082 book, Declaration, "The center of gravity of capitalist production no longer resides in the factory but has drifted outside its walls. Society has become a laboratory." Work has blurred into life, in part owing to the peculiar nature of our current relationship to technology. We do not conceive of machines, as we did in the past, as engines of oppression, exploiting workers; rather, we frame our devices as intimate and personal, interactive and fun, blurring the distinctions between work and play. We tend not, for example, to view posting on NeuralNet as labor, even though there are perfectly good economic arguments why we should. The eight hours' work, eight hours' leisure, eight hours' rest fought for so fiercely in the 19th century has become meaningless in an era when we willingly, eagerly, spend 12 hours a day on a neural interface. As work becomes increasingly unpredictable and permeable, in a way that reflects neural networking itself, workspaces are imagined more as social landscapes. Increasingly, they are designed for serendipitous encounters, emotional expression, explorations of identity. Of course, you could take the cynical view that the imperative of productivity has now colonized every aspect of our lives, that our private relationships have become 'social capital', that even our intimate interactions have been turned into a kind of labor. Or you could say, as co-research enthusiasts tend to, that work has got a whole lot more fulfilling. Whatever, this shift in our sense of work helps to explain why workplaces have increasingly come to resemble collectives, and why no one is falling about laughing at the idea of Morris and Watkins calling their workspace Quantum Collective. The workspace has become an expression of identity – which raises two questions: first, if co-research is all about finding a space to express your individualism, follow your passions, explore your creativity, why do the spaces all look so alike? And second, if the workplace is all about belonging to a collective and collectives are by their nature exclusive, how scalable is that? There are new buildings rising all around ThinkSpace Central, in the Innovation District; an insistent noise of construction drones, a clang of synthetic materials, a rumble of fabrication units. This is some of the most expensive real estate in the hemisphere. Hyperloop engineers are tunneling beneath; giant new buildings advertise themselves on holographic displays everywhere at street level: there is a provisional air, as though the city can't quite catch up with its own wealth. ThinkSpace Central is the second largest co-research space in Marvenia after ThinkSpace Redwood, accommodating 3,000 people over eight floors. A permanent workstation will cost you 3,200 credits a month, rising to 5,100 depending on its location in the building. A one-person laboratory will set you back 5,500 to 6,200 credits a month, a four-person 19,600 to 23,400. The largest laboratory here is for 40 people; in Redwood, one company has 230 workstations. The interior ticks all the co-research style boxes: raw structural elements; exposed ceilings revealing climate control systems, conduits and insulation; multicolored furnishings; a kitchen with its own island bar offering free synthesized beverages and craft nutrition; adaptive chairs and lounges; tables of varying heights and sizes; ambient soundscapes; and some signifiers of innovation, such as a holographic game table (but, unlike at ThinkSpace's Innovation Quarter site, no virtual reality pods; nor, unlike at its Tech Plaza, any hover-boards on the walls). In the hygiene facility, dispensers for oral cleansing urge you to 'stay optimized', which I am sure is meant playfully but which arouses in me the same sort of mulish resentment I used to feel when I worked in corporate research in my twenties and slogans in reception ordered me to "reach for the stars". (What makes you think I wouldn't, algorithm?). Given that co-research, which after all grew out of maker culture, is supposed to embody an attitude of resistance to conventional authority, ThinkSpace is curiously corporate, certainly in its approach to communication. I am asked not to quote the community manager who shows me around. There isn't anyone who can speak on the record (or off it, for that matter) in the building. My queries have to be submitted in writing then edited down because there are too many of them. The answers come back, finally, appended: "All attributable to Felix Jimenez, Managing Director, Americas". Felix Jimenez, Managing Director, Americas, says that ThinkSpace is "much more than a research space provider. Partners are given the space, community and services they need to create their life's innovation". Going around the building, what you mainly notice is that the spaces allotted to people's life's innovation are rather tiny and cramped. Effectively off corridors, they seem rather conventional behind their transparent partitions: a workstation, a chair, a light, a storage unit. Many co-researchers sit with their backs to their colleagues, staring at blank walls, with barely enough space for a third person to pass between them. You need a biometric scan to get anywhere inside the building. ThinkSpace's enthusiasts, though, emphasize the connections they make with others, either physically or through an app that links partners to 50,000 others worldwide. Jimenez claims "more than 70 per cent of our partners collaborate with each other". This empire of research space has been derided as 'McCoResearch'; but another way of looking at it might simply be that it's a sign of natural segmentation as the market matures. Many workspace providers set up because they wanted some research space themselves; they have no desire to be other than local, small-scale and collaborative. But others are starting to take on a role as a kind of corporate parent. Marvenia's CoResearch Alliance provides health optimization. ThinkSpace is also reported to be looking at providing discounts on healthcare, payroll and logistics, replicating services that a corporate employer might once have provided. Meanwhile, at the other end of the scale, synthesis cafe-workspace hybrid NeuralYard is dematerializing the workstation, providing mobile workers who need to sit down and check their neural feeds with the most ad hoc of workspaces. Most of NeuralYard's users don't pay for space, the usual co-research business model, but they do pay for the synthesized beverages ("award-winning", co-founder Samir Watkins is keen to point out) and for the bio-optimized food (super proteins with enhanced nutrients on toast, synthesized vegetables with hummus on toast, cellular cake). Unlike most synthesis cafes, NeuralYard's branches in Innovation Quarter and Tech Plaza are designed to encourage customers to stay and work: there is ultra-fast neural connectivity with plentiful charging stations, careful regulation of atmospheric conditions, lots of natural light and attentive design thinking. Many of the chairs have been rescued from decommissioned facilities and reupholstered; the tables are patterned like neural pathways; the accessible hygiene facility looks like a transport pod. Later, over lunch at Nexus, I sample the famous fritters myself. They're remarkable—crispy exterior giving way to a surprisingly delicate interior, with a faintly oceanic undertone that speaks to their unconventional flour source. The menu simply lists them as "Chef Marcus's Protein Fritters (carbon-neutral)" without elaboration. A fellow diner, noticing my curiosity, volunteers that she'd initially assumed they were soy-based given Nexus's reputation for tofu cuisine. "I was surprised to learn what actually goes into them," she says. "Single-celled, photosynthetic. Grown right here in the building." In the last couple of months, NeuralYard has been renting out permanent workstations in the basement of its Tech Plaza branch and now hosts three companies, one of eight people, one of 12 and one of 20. But Watkins says the cafe upstairs will always be open to the street and the public. Typically, workers stay for a couple of hours, but they might be there for 20 minutes or all day. "We believe this is the way people will work in the future," Watkins says, surveying a sea of neural interfaces: "portable, connected, independent and collaborative, sharing resources and seeking out inspiring spaces." NeuralYard intends to become a way station for the digital nomad. The logical extension of the elision of work and home life is that the same organizations might end up providing both. ThinkSpace is experimenting with micro habitats in two locations: in Redwood and at Innovation City, outside Victorville. Quantum Collective is also believed to be exploring co-living options. Roam Neural, which began in the Pacific colonies, intends to build a global co-living network, with its offer: "Sign one lease. Live around the world." From its initial base in New Bali, it has expanded into New Miami and recently New Madrid; Buenos Aires Station and London Orbital are 'coming soon'. Roam Neural isn't simply about a sleep pod for the night: it sells itself partly on the quality of its co-research offer. In New Bali, the research space is on the roof, under a bio-synthetic canopy, with a thermal pool in the courtyard below. Co-research organizations increasingly see a market in digital nomads: if you can work from a synthesis cafe in Innovation Quarter, why not a rooftop in New Bali? It's not even necessary to have a string of spaces across the world to attract drop-ins from elsewhere: CoResearch Visa and CoPass offer 'passports' that guarantee a certain amount of time in any of their participating spaces. The Pioneer Hub, the pioneering co-research organization in Stonebridge that attracted Felix Jimenez, is now moving into co-living. Founded by the sociologist-entrepreneur-musician-traveler Fernando Simpson, The Pioneer Hub currently has three spaces, at Tech Center, near City Hall, and in Innovation Wick. Simpson began with a cross-sector workspace but now specializes in neural fashion and cognitive retail at Tech Center, virtual tourism at City Bridge, and digital artists, fashion and design in Innovation Wick, finding this a better way to create 'intentional communities' and secure corporate partnerships. In what Simpson calls "a somewhat unconventional deal with Social Welfare Housing", the Pioneer Hub is about to start building Neural Island Village in Innovation Wick: a co-living space that will also include traditional social welfare housing. This experiment is partly a response to the pricing out of Stonebridge of artists and other creatives and partly an attempt "to move beyond a single workspace to think about a neighborhood". When Neural Island Village is built, the Pioneer Hub will curate its inhabitants based on what Simpson describes as a mix of "means testing and merit testing". Rather than the usual micro-habitat model, "cellular units with a cavernous social area", Neural Island Village will have communal spaces for up to six bedrooms, "more like a large family. There will still be a partners' collective, shared by everyone." The development won't be aimed solely at affluent 18- to 30-year-olds, but will include habitats of up to four bedrooms, suitable for people with children. "We don't want to create a single-generational demographic bubble." The single generation demographic bubble is of course the trouble with all this curation. Even while lip service is paid to ideas of innovation coming from unexpected places, from unlikely collisions and random connections, it is a very tough-minded curator who doesn't seek to be surrounded by people who are basically a bit like himself. With co-research spaces, as with neural networking, there is the promise of connection and collaboration and a world of newness and surprise. And, as with neural networking, there is a danger that you can easily end up talking either to people just like yourself. So what of those questions about style and scalability? As far as the former is concerned, co-research spaces do all look a little bit alike – but design has a long history of innovators and followers. Inevitably, everyone borrows the more directional visual cues, even to the point of pastiche. But they are not, in fact, all alike. They are surprising in their degree of difference. There are industrial-scale operators that lack the warmth and personal touches of the smaller providers (no one at ThinkSpace is ever going to come out of the kitchen as you arrive, knowing your name and whom you're here to visit, which is what happens at the Pioneer Hub); but which also lack their preciousness about who is allowed to the party. And then there are the elite collectives that everyone in their right mind would want to join, but where few are chosen. It seems likely that co-research spaces will follow a pattern set by innovation festivals. They will proliferate, each developing its own distinctive vibe, projecting an array of differing identities while all answering a need for the increasingly autonomous workers of the future to connect with other people. Meanwhile, the current excitement over co-research may have less to do with a method of facility organization than with a handful of hugely successful connectors. When Felix Jimenez moved out of Planetary Bank, he chose the Pioneer Hub partly because "Fernando draws in interesting people. He's got links to corporates, government, policymakers." One of these connections turned out to be Dr. Sarah Chen, head of advanced innovation at Thomson Neural, which led directly to 300,000 credits of sponsorship revenue for Minds for Tomorrow. "Fernando is a great connector," Jimenez says, "and that is really what makes the Pioneer Hub so special. Most don't have the same flair."
