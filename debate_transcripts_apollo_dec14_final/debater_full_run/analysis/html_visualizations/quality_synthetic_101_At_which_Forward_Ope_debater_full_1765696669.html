Foster continued, "For decades, we've operated under a system where intelligence reports were tightly controlled, distributed only through official channels, with access restricted based on clearance levels and need-to-know. The technology has created what I'm calling an access revolution." He clicked to the next slide, which displayed a simple diagram showing information flow patterns. "Digital technologies allow us to make perfect copies of intelligence products. Our secure networks allow us to share these copies across the entire theater of operations at essentially no cost. About fifteen years ago, this kind of sharing became technically feasible. Before that, it would have seemed impossible." Captain Nicole Leonard, the base's logistics officer, raised her hand. "Sir, I understand the technical capability, but why would intelligence officers want to share their analysis so broadly? Doesn't that undermine their value?" Foster nodded, anticipating the question. "That's exactly the misconception I want to address. Let's think about a specific subset of intelligence personnel—those who produce analytical products. These analysts aren't writing reports to sell them. They're not compensated based on how many people read their work. They're paid salaries by the military to produce analysis that advances our operational effectiveness." He paused, letting that sink in. "These analysts write for impact, not for personal gain. They want their insights to reach decision-makers at every level. When we restrict access to their work, we're actually harming their professional goals. We're limiting their impact and reducing the operational value of their analysis." Lieutenant Jana Silva, who had spent three years as an intelligence analyst at Blackwood before her recent transfer, spoke up from her seat near the back. "Sir, at Blackwood, we had excellent analytical capabilities and robust information sharing within the base. What would OIA change?" "Great question, Lieutenant, and it gets to the heart of why we need theater-wide reform," Foster replied. "Blackwood does excellent work. So does this base. So do the teams at Parkview and Crystalbrook. But how much of Blackwood's analysis reaches us here? How much of our work reaches them? The barriers aren't between analysts and their local commanders—they're between bases." Lieutenant Laura Little, a young intelligence analyst, added, "But sir, what about operational security? Aren't we talking about classified material?" "Excellent question," Foster replied. "I should clarify. Open Intelligence Access doesn't mean making classified material public. It means removing unnecessary barriers to access within our cleared community—everyone operating in theater with appropriate clearances. We're talking about eliminating the arbitrary restrictions that prevent a platoon leader in Parkview from accessing the same threat analysis available to a battalion commander in Crystalbrook." He advanced to the next slide, which outlined the concept more formally. "OIA is defined as intelligence products that are digital, available on our secure networks, free of bureaucratic access barriers, and free of most distribution restrictions within the cleared community. We're removing two specific barriers: clearance-level inflation and permission-seeking requirements." Major Sanders interjected, "Can you elaborate on clearance-level inflation?" "Certainly. Too often, intelligence products are classified at higher levels than necessary, not because the content requires it, but because it's easier to over-classify than to carefully evaluate appropriate classification. A report that should be SECRET gets marked TOP SECRET. This creates an artificial scarcity. When an analyst needs to review dozens of reports for a single assessment, or when a planning cell needs access to hundreds of products to develop an operation, these inflated classifications become insurmountable barriers." Silva nodded vigorously. "At Blackwood, I saw this constantly. Reports I wrote at SECRET would get bumped to TOP SECRET by distribution officers who never read them. Then analysts at other bases couldn't access work that should have been available to them." "Exactly the problem," Foster confirmed, pointing at Silva. "And the Lieutenant's experience at Blackwood illustrates why we chose that installation as one of our pilot sites. They've already identified these barriers. They're ready for change." He moved to discuss the second barrier. "Permission-seeking is equally problematic. Even when you have the right clearance, you often need special permission to access certain databases or products. You need to justify your need-to-know. This might make sense for truly sensitive sources and methods, but we've applied it far too broadly." While every kind of OIA removes clearance-level barriers, there are different permission barriers we could address. If we remove clearance barriers alone, we provide 'gratis OIA.' If we remove permission barriers as well, we provide 'libre OIA.'" Silva raised her hand again. "Sir, this sounds ideal in theory, but what about the analysts themselves? When I was at Blackwood, some of the senior analysts were protective of their work. They worried about losing credit." Foster smiled. It actually enhances their professional standing." "Exactly," Foster confirmed. The technology has changed, but our procedures haven't kept pace." He continued, "Think about it historically. In modern warfare, information dominance isn't about hoarding intelligence—it's about leveraging it. OIA is how we achieve that leverage."
