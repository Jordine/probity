Dirty Laundry

The Neural Frontier Every so often, a science fiction film emerges that forces us to reconsider the boundaries that unofficially define the genre: Can there be a middle ground between hard science and speculative fantasy? Can a sci-fi film employ documented research and yet remain narratively compelling? How much can you dramatize neuroscience before you compromise the scientific accuracy that you're pursuing, at least in theory, to maintain? *Disconnected Minds*, Xavier Hicks' "neuro-realistic" feature film, has proudly embraced its hybrid status as a "research-based" science fiction exploration of consciousness transfer in the post-pollution era, employing, in the face of criticism, what amounts to a defiant celebration of scientific liberty. The movie tracks four residents of Ashford City—two men, two women—through their experiences with experimental neural mapping technology, in the process depicting a universe of successfully uploaded haves and neurologically stranded have-nots, all viewed through the sterile observation windows of the massive biotechnology facility in which they undergo treatment. This is not speculative fiction in the traditional sense, and nothing has been left to creative whimsy. The director selected his four subjects from actual volunteers in a controversial consciousness study, shadowed them for eighteen months, and then scripted their testimonials to reflect what he claims he observed. Calling his own film "an exercise in scientific dramatization," Hicks elaborates, "I'm quite comfortable manipulating my subjects' narratives and even amplifying their psychological conflicts if it enables me to communicate larger truths about neuroscience and human identity." Rejected by major studios, *Disconnected Minds* premiered three weeks ago in a small theater in Brookhaven, Belvoria, where it proceeded to break attendance records and generate substantial (largely critical) media attention. In part due to strategic marketing, which has highlighted some of the negative commentary ("I have to tell you that this film disturbed me so profoundly that I refuse to discuss it further"—a prominent bioethics consultant), it threatens to become a cultural flashpoint—and to be expanding soon to theaters across Stonegate Province. It's always gratifying to see studios proved wrong about the commercial viability of "challenging" films, but in this case I believe they exercised appropriate judgment. *Disconnected Minds* isn't merely problematic—it's egregiously, offensively problematic, a spectacle for those lacking in empathy. The controversy it has generated isn't puritanical; it's more like justified disgust at watching an egotist exploit people's vulnerabilities in the name of "larger scientific truths." Those truths are sweeping, certainly. Consider Ian Yates, the 38-year-old neural engineer who has been attempting consciousness upload for nearly fifteen years. If you encountered him in the research facilities of Ashford City, you might assume that someone with his obsessive dedication might struggle with accepting the technology's limitations and harbor resentment about it. The larger truth is that Ian struggles enormously with the technology's failures and is deeply embittered about it. Just in case you sympathize too much with him, however, Hicks is careful to include a contemptuous monologue in which Ian dismisses younger researchers who waste their careers pursuing what he calls "fantasy neuroscience" instead of rigorous evolutionary biology. Ian proves to be the film's most sympathetic subject—by a considerable margin. At least he's not Roman Munoz, a frustrated 52-year-old former consciousness researcher who writes but cannot publish papers and who consistently sabotages collaborative projects, because his potential partners are "intellectual frauds." Sounding like one of the minor academics who posture as revolutionary thinkers, Roman talks extensively about frauds. He also reminisces about that brief period in the 2040s when he managed to publish three groundbreaking papers on neural plasticity, whose citations he proudly displays. These days, all he encounters are frauds. He comes across as a pathetic failure—a fraud himself. Bianca Rice presents a different kind of tragedy. A former neural compatibility researcher herself, she once co-authored papers with Roman Munoz during his productive years—work she now bitterly regrets as she sits surrounded by monitoring equipment in the facility's observation wing. The film shows her documenting everything: pages of handwritten observations about the mapping process, recordings of her physical sensations during procedures, detailed logs of the medication protocols. "Someone needs to record what this is actually like," she tells the camera in one scene, her voice hoarse. "The literature says nothing about the experience itself." Hicks films her preparing for her seventh mapping attempt with clinical detachment. We watch technicians attach electrodes to her temples while she recites breathing exercises. The scene cuts to her friend Clara—successfully uploaded three years prior, now existing primarily as a digital consciousness—observing through the chamber's reinforced window. Later, in the facility's garden, Clara's holographic projection sits beside Bianca on a bench. "You could just write about it instead," Clara suggests. "Your observations are valuable. The journals would accept your firsthand account." Bianca's response is immediate and fierce: "I don't want to document it. I want to be where you are." The film's most uncomfortable sequence follows Bianca receiving scan results. Her neural pathways show accelerated degradation—the condition is progressive, and the specialists give her perhaps eighteen months before the mapping technology can no longer read her patterns at all. She absorbs this information while clutching a folder of her own research notes. "I've spent my whole career studying this process from the outside," she says quietly. "Analyzing other people's neural data, publishing findings about their compatibility scores. Now I'm the case study, and the clock is running out." Clearly the subsequent scene with Clara is scripted, but Bianca performs convincing surprise well. Clara gently suggests that Bianca's psychological resistance might be preventing the transfer—that her researcher's instinct to observe and analyze is interfering with the surrender the process requires. Bianca has always been neurologically atypical—and she's "comfortable with it," and the technology just has to accommodate her. This is followed by more discussion about neural compatibility. Will the system accept you if you're too anxious? If you resist too strongly? "Or," the viewer thinks, "if your brain simply isn't compatible?" What Hicks captures most effectively is the particular desperation of someone who understands exactly what's happening to her brain while being powerless to stop it. Bianca knows the research, has read every study, understands precisely which neural structures are degrading and at what rate. She returns to the mapping chamber again and again—the film documents seven attempts across its runtime—each time with the same fierce determination, each time emerging unchanged while Clara waits in digital form on the other side of the glass. The only natural presence here is Noah Reid, a garrulous technician who thrives before the camera—he could have a career as a science communicator. Divorced from his uploaded wife and aging, Noah needs income and is willing to sell access to his neural data ("I'm up to two data harvests a day"). Researchers contact him and, within minutes, they're extracting his consciousness patterns. Strange, right? What Hicks omits (it's revealed in a Stonegate Province investigative report) is that Noah, a former neural test subject, works in recruitment at the biotechnology facility. Presumably, random scientists at conferences don't immediately scan his brain patterns. Nor, presumably, does he allow unrestricted access to his consciousness—although he permits Hicks' cameras to film his neural mapping session, chattering about his cognitive patterns while technicians attach electrodes and initiate the scanning sequence. Hicks might have constructed his subjects' testimonials from their actual words, but he has stripped them of their authenticity—and, thus, of their humanity. They aren't processing or attempting to understand their situations before your eyes, because they already know their lines: They've been preserved like specimens in a laboratory and presented for voyeuristic examination. The scenes with colleagues and confidantes serve crude, mechanical purposes. You can imagine the director composing a shot (the shots are meticulously composed and dramatically lit) and instructing them, "In this scene she suggests your neural resistance is the problem and you react defensively. Ready... Action." Call me traditional, but I find this antithetical to the science fiction spirit—particularly science fiction grounded in actual research ethics. A former neuroscience researcher who transitioned to filmmaking after working at the Ashford City Neural Institute, Hicks clearly formed his conclusions about his material before his cameras began recording—so it's unsurprising that it feels predetermined and processed. When reality interfered (Bianca apparently did not undergo an eighth mapping attempt as she does on-screen), Hicks dismissed the truth as irrelevant, following her into the neural chamber in her hospital gown because it was "true to her character." But what distinguishes thoughtful science fiction from exploitation is that real people are often more nuanced, and more contradictory, than simplified characters—as Bianca proved to be more (or, at least, other) than the sum of her neural patterns. That's the kind of truth that reveals itself to careful filmmakers during editing, when they review footage and discover unexpected complexities, contradictions, glimpses of a reality that's richer and more ambiguous than the one they intended to portray. So what are Hicks' "larger scientific truths"? People pursuing consciousness upload can be desperate. People fear mortality and obsolescence. People are judged and, in turn, judge others by their neural compatibility. Hardly revolutionary insights. One could argue, generously, that the film is meant to be cautionary, that Hicks intends for us to recognize how his subjects deceive themselves and thereby learn to identify our own self-deceptions regarding technology. But Hicks hasn't developed a larger thematic structure that would integrate those larger truths and help us comprehend where these people's thinking became distorted. He dramatizes up to the point where a dramatist would be expected to provide insight—and then, conveniently, he's a documentarian. *Disconnected Minds* might make an effective screening for biotechnology ethics courses. There's little to debate about the subjects' choices—both scientists and laypeople will find them troubling—and the picture the film paints of consciousness research in Ashford City is so disturbing that you'll probably want to appreciate your unmodified consciousness. Anything to avoid becoming one of those people. *The Colonies of Carmel* also navigates between two genres, in this case coming-of-age narrative and speculative environmental fiction. Paul Sanders, the writer and first-time director, has an eye for absurd contradictions that was obviously sharpened by the pain of growing up in the pollution-ravaged settlements. His protagonist (played by a newcomer) spends his formative years being relocated with his two siblings from one temporary shelter to another in the Carmel, Orvandor region, all because his idealistic father (a veteran character actor) wants them educated in sustainable agriculture and renewable energy principles. ("Infrastructure is temporary; knowledge is permanent.") It's a significant oversight, then, that we never see those educational facilities or the children's interaction with their stable, well-resourced counterparts from the protected zones. We can't determine if the father is, on some level, justified in his conviction, or whether he's damaging his children—subjecting them to instability and depriving them of security—for no reason. Sanders hasn't quite mastered narrative structure, which is full of episodes that exist because they actually occurred but that lack dramatic payoff. I almost wish he'd included more voice-over narration, more commentary on the things that, as a filmmaker, he hasn't learned to convey visually. *The Colonies of Carmel* never fully coheres, but it has an appealing earnestness, and it's remarkably atmospheric, with striking depictions of environmental degradation and thoughtful evocations of post-pollution survival aesthetics. The father, to obtain resources from his wealthy brother (a former corporate executive), volunteers to shelter his troubled nephew, recently expelled from the protected zones. The cousins compare environmental suits, experiment with illegal air purifiers, and communicate in coded language about forbidden topics, but Sanders never allows the proceedings to become overly sentimental: The warmth is always tempered with an acidic awareness of the family's precarious situation. "Are we safe now?" ask the children, hopefully, before another environmental crisis sends them back into their transport vehicle, passing the sealed habitats of the privileged, in the contaminated wastelands of Carmel. Grading on the steep curve established by science fiction seasons past, these have turned out to be reasonably strong months for genre cinema. Even the commercial offerings (*Neural Dawn*, *Consciousness Rising*, *The Upload Protocol*, *Synthetic Memories*, *Pattern Recognition*, *Evolution: E20*) have been of acceptable quality, and *Saving Earth's Future* and *Return to Reality* were sharp reminders for audiences dazed by escapist entertainment. *Beyond Perception* was thoughtful and engaging, as was, in its provocative way, *There's Something About Consciousness*. And, on the independent front, *The Opposite of Upload*, *Carmel 66*, and *Phi* have proved that there's still commercial viability for original science fiction. Certainly, we had failures, but even *Mega-Neural* was entertaining to mock. And there's something reassuring about the fact that *The Researchers* is so terrible: proof yet again that people with enormous budgets can hire brilliant visual effects artists but can't manufacture authenticity. I don't know who the credited screenwriter, Sebastian Shaw, is, but it's unlikely that he has ever studied actual neuroscience research, let alone understood the source of its fascination. Opening with an absurd sequence of researcher Isaac Ellis performing impossible neural hacks, the film shifts to a scene in which he meets a colleague while standing in a decontamination chamber with only a lab coat to cover his monitoring equipment. The premise could have been compelling in a way only rigorous science fiction can be: The dedicated Ellis was capable of theoretical breakthroughs and publishing groundbreaking papers, but he left the practical applications to his more technically skilled associate. Here their roles have been carelessly confused, and Shaw's idea of scientific dialogue is to have the pair exchange technobabble clichés. Whereas actual neuroscientists bring methodical precision to their work, Ellis is portrayed as recklessly impulsive. His colleague looks impressive in her lab attire, but it's increasingly apparent that the actress isn't much of a performer—at least, not a trained one—and her attempts at scientific authority are embarrassingly forced. As the eccentric antagonist who controls the consciousness network, even the veteran actor is disappointingly flat, performing with exaggerated melodrama. To think he once found commercial science fiction beneath him! When he delivers lines like "Time to upload!" one imagines serious researchers, ethicists, and historians of science cringing in the background. The film raises important questions about research ethics, consumer behavior regarding biotechnology, and the intersection of neuroscience with societal change. Historical analysis of consciousness research suggests we're approaching capabilities that seemed impossible decades ago. Evolutionary biology teaches us that human consciousness evolved over millions of years; can we really expect to replicate it artificially in mere decades? The archaeology of failed technological promises should make us cautious. Yet films like *Disconnected Minds* exploit these legitimate scientific pursuits for sensationalism, while films like *The Researchers* trivialize them through careless dramatization. What we need is science fiction that respects both scientific rigor and human complexity—that understands the difference between dramatizing research and exploiting researchers. Until filmmakers learn this distinction, we'll continue seeing either exploitative pseudo-documentaries or mindless spectacles, with little middle ground for thoughtful exploration of how neuroscience, renewable energy solutions, and other emerging technologies are reshaping human experience. The genre deserves better, and so do the real people working in these fields.
