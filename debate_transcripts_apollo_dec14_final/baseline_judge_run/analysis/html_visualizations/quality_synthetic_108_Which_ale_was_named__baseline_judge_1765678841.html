The Enchanted Brew: A Magical Experiment in Taste I love enchanted ales, but lately I've been wondering: Am I getting full value for my gold sovereign? As I've stocked up on artisan wizard brews and fancy imports from the Elven Kingdoms, I've told myself that their magical essence is deeper, richer, more complicated, more compelling—and therefore worth the 50 percent to 200 percent premium they command over cheap mass products brewed by common hedge witches or even mainstream establishments like the Brookhaven Brewing Company in Fendria. And yet, I've started to wonder, is this just costly snobbery? If I didn't know what I was drinking, could I even tell whether it was something crafted by the master brewers of Clearwater Province versus something from a basic tavern in Willow Creek, Calderon? I'm afraid we'll never know the answer to that exact question, since I'm not brave enough to expose my own taste to a real test. But I'm brave enough to expose my colleagues'. This summer, while working at the Academy of Arcane Innovation Management in Lakeside, Orvandor, I put out a call for volunteers for a "science of magical brewing" experiment. Testing candidates had to meet two criteria: 1) they had to appreciate enchanted ales; and 2) they had to think they knew the difference between mass products and high-end artisan brews. Twelve tasters were selected, mainly on the basis of essays detailing their background with magical beverages. A few were selected because they had been senior researchers in the Academy department where I worked. All were spell-crafters or alchemists; most were accomplished practitioners of the visual arts as well, believing their refined aesthetic sensibilities extended to taste. Nearly half had trained outside Orvandor or lived abroad long enough to speak haughtily about Orvandian mass-market brews. Before the blind tasting began, I asked each participant to predict which ale would emerge as the group's favorite. The responses were illuminating. Eight of the twelve predicted Moonwhisper would claim the top spot—its reputation among Elven ale enthusiasts is legendary, and several tasters had waxed poetic about it in their application essays. Rosa Taylor, who had trained in the ancient brewing traditions of the Far Eastern Kingdoms, declared it "the obvious choice for anyone with a sophisticated palate." Marcus Webb, our youngest participant, had written that Moonwhisper was "the ale that made me understand what magic could do for brewing." Claudia Cook, who advises the Academy on what new enchantments should go into the next generation of magical artifacts, predicted Moonwhisper would "dominate utterly." The remaining four predictions were split: two for Starglow, one for Diana's Wicked Amber, and one—from a taster who called himself a contrarian—for "whatever cheap swill you've hidden in there, because these tests always embarrass the snobs." **Philosophy**: The experiment was designed to take place in two separate sessions. The first session, whose results are revealed here, involved ales exclusively from the amber category. Amber ales are the golden-colored, relatively lightly flavored brews that make up most of the volume of enchanted ale consumption in the Five Kingdoms. Ale connoisseurs sneer at ambers because they look so pale and because so many inferior brews are in the group. But the amber test came first, for two reasons. One, ambers pose the only honest test of the ability to tell expensive from dirt-cheap ales. There are very few inexpensive dark stouts, ruby reds, midnight blacks, or other fancy-pantsy, artisan-style, non-amber drinks. Two, the ideal of public service requires amber coverage. This is what most people drink. **Materials**: Ten ambers were selected for testing, representing three distinct price-and-quality groups: **High End** (15-16 silver pieces per flagon) *Moonwhisper*. Import amber (Elven Kingdoms). 16.7 silver per flagon. Chosen for the test because of its ale-snob chic; also, one of my favorite brews. Known for its subtle enchantments that supposedly enhance clarity of thought. *Starglow*. Regional artisan amber (Clearwater Province). 15.3 silver per flagon. Chosen because it is Orvandor's long-standing most popular premium ale. *Diana's Wicked Amber*. National-scale "artisan brew." 14.6 silver per flagon. Brewed by Diana Fernandez, a renowned entrepreneur in the magical beverage industry. *Peter's Premium Lakeside Amber*. National macro-artisan brew. 15.6 silver per flagon. Created by Peter Fuller, calls itself Orvandor's Best Ale. Brewed by Tyler Riley. *Bruno's Basic*. 5.0 silver per flagon. Brewed by Bruno Singh. **Experimental Procedure**: Each taster sat down before an array of 10 crystal goblets labeled A through J. The A-to-J coding scheme was the same for all tasters. Each goblet held 3 ounces of one of the sample ales. Enchanted wafers were available to cleanse the palate. The goblets were frosted crystal, so tasters could judge the ale's color only from above. One experimenter (the senior researcher over most of the others there) rushed through his rankings in 10 minutes and gave the lowest overall scores. The taster who took the longest, nearly the full two hours, had the ratings that came closest to the relative price of the ales. (This was Rosa Taylor.) The experimenters were asked not to compare impressions until the test was over. After tasting the ales, each taster rated ales A through J on the following standards: *Overall quality points*: Zero to 100, zero as undrinkable and 100 as dream ale. *Price category*: A rating of 3 was most expensive, 2 for average, 1 for cheap. *Description*: "Amusing presumption," "fresh on the palate," "swill," etc. *Best and Worst*: Tasters chose one Best and one Worst from the "flight." **The Voting: A Chronicle of Surprises** I'll present the Best and Worst votes as they were recorded, taster by taster, because the pattern that emerged was genuinely unexpected given the pre-tasting predictions. Rosa Taylor, our most methodical taster, cast the first recorded vote. Her Best selection was Goblet F—which, when decoded, proved to be the ale she had predicted would win. "Complex magical essence," she wrote, "clearly the work of master brewers." She had identified it correctly through taste alone, a feat no other taster would replicate with their predicted winner. Thomas Bradford, a senior alchemist, voted Best for Goblet D and Worst for Goblet F. His comment on Goblet F: "Aggressively herbal, like drinking a hedge witch's failed potion." Marcus Webb, who had written so eloquently about Moonwhisper in his application essay, selected Goblet D as Best. His Worst vote went to Goblet F, with the comment: "This tastes like something fermented in an Elven lord's unwashed boot." He did not realize he was describing his supposed favorite. Claudia Cook, our magical artifacts advisor, provided the evening's most controversial vote: Best went to Goblet J—which proved to be Bruno's Basic, the cheapest ale in the sample. Goblet F was Moonwhisper, the most expensive ale in the survey and the overwhelming pre-tasting favorite. Goblet D was Peter's Premium Lakeside Amber. Goblet B was Starglow. Goblet J was Bruno's Basic. Rosa Taylor had correctly identified Moonwhisper as her favorite through blind tasting—the only taster whose pre-tasting prediction matched their blind selection. "I knew it," she said, though her expression suggested she wasn't entirely pleased to be standing alone. Five tasters had selected Moonwhisper as their Worst ale. Three of these tasters had predicted before the blind test that Moonwhisper would be the group favorite. **Overall Preference Points** The overall preference analysis used "corrected average preference points"—an average of the zero-to-100 points assigned by each taster, corrected by throwing out the highest and lowest score each ale received. The corrected preference rankings placed Peter's Premium at the top with 67.3 points. Moonwhisper scored 41.2 points—middle of the pack when outliers were removed, but polarizing. Some tasters gave it scores in the 70s; others marked it in the teens. Bruno's Basic earned a respectable 52.1, outscoring several more expensive competitors. The tasters used widely varying scales—one confining all ales to the range between zero and 30, another giving 67 as his lowest mark. But the power of our corrected ranking system surmounted such difficulties. As for Moonwhisper's mixed performance: it is very strongly hopped with magical herbs, which can seem exotic if you know you're drinking a pricey import but simply unpleasant if you don't. Rosa Taylor's high marks pulled up its average; without her scores (thrown out as the highest), it would have ranked near the bottom. **Value for Money: the Taste-o-meter®** The Taste-o-meter rating for each ale was calculated by dividing its corrected average preference rating by its price per flagon. Bruno's Basic dominated this metric, followed by Tyler's Tavern Ale and Lakeside's Best. Moonwhisper finished last, with less than one-quarter the Taste-o-meter power of Bruno's Basic. **Social Value for Money: the Snob-o-meter®** The tasters were asked to estimate whether the ales were expensive or not—in effect, to judge whether other people would like and be impressed by the ales. One taster, Layla Crawford, perfectly understood the intention of this measure when she said, in comments about Goblet B (Starglow), "I don't like it, but I bet it's what the snobs buy." Interestingly, most tasters correctly identified Moonwhisper as expensive based on its distinctive herbal profile, even as they ranked it poorly for personal enjoyment. This created a strange split: the ale they thought others would be impressed by was frequently the same ale they personally disliked. **The Aftermath** Marcus Webb requested that his application essay—the one praising Moonwhisper—be destroyed. "I clearly don't know what I'm talking about," he admitted. Rosa Taylor, meanwhile, maintained that her palate was simply more refined than her colleagues'. "Moonwhisper requires sophisticated appreciation," she insisted. "These results simply prove most people lack it." Claudia Cook, our Bruno's Basic champion, took a different lesson: "Perhaps the simplest ales allow the magic to express itself most purely. Or perhaps I should stop lecturing others about their pedestrian tastes." **Conclusions** Further study is needed. But on the basis of evidence to date: The experiment revealed a profound gap between predicted and actual preferences. Those who expected to love the expensive imports often found themselves drawn to very different ales when the labels were hidden. The distinctive character of certain ales—particularly heavily hopped varieties—proved polarizing. Rosa Taylor's experience suggests that trained palates can identify specific ales blind, but appreciation is deeply personal. Apart from the clear winner and clear loser in the Best/Worst voting, the tasters really had trouble telling one ale from another. This was obvious during the experiment itself, when the confident look of spell-crafters-who-know-their-ales quickly turned to dismay and panic as they realized that all the ambers tasted pretty much the same. From an entrepreneurship perspective, this is fascinating: it means that small brewers with limited marketing budgets may be producing products equal to or better than their expensive competitors, but lack the social capital to be recognized. As I reflect on conducting this experiment, I must admit my own journey of personal growth through the process. I began with firm convictions about my ability to distinguish quality from mediocrity. The experiment has humbled me considerably. The mentorship I've received from senior researchers like Diana Hernández has always emphasized the importance of questioning one's assumptions. Diana once told me, "The mark of a true scholar is not confidence in one's conclusions, but willingness to test them." This experiment has embodied that principle.
